## 1. Purpose and reading method

Extract business-building capabilities from all five supplied books while preserving what each author actually argues. This is neither a substitute edition of the books nor empirical validation of their methods. The [Stage 1 charter](2026-09-09-stage-01-domain-and-professional-boundary.md) remains the domain and professional boundary.

Three kinds of statement are kept separate:

- **Source:** an author's definition, recommendation, reported experience or illustrative calculation. A locator supports attribution, not independent truth.
- **Project analysis:** an inferred workflow implication, failure condition or reconciliation under the bootstrap principles. These are not silently attributed to the author.
- **Validation question:** a claim requiring independent professional or empirical research in Stage 3. Its presence is not evidence that research has already been performed.

All five complete PDFs were accessible and their native text was extracted page by page. Contents, relevant method sections, qualifications, metric definitions, contrary passages and selected figures were inspected. Reading was directed by capability coverage and the eleven required extraction fields; this is not a claim that every page received an equally close reading. Native extraction and document search were used together. No OCR was necessary. Text extraction alone was not accepted as evidence that a figure had been read.

Only the supplied copies, the original specification, execution contract and accepted Stage 1 records support this stage. No outside study, customer interview, market dataset, live experiment or current-law review was conducted. The three Hormozi volumes share an author, examples and commercial context; they are not three independent replications. Cross-references and common intellectual lineage also limit independence between the other books.

## 2. O: Offers extraction

### business problem addressed

**Source:** Undifferentiated offers invite price comparison while acquisition and fulfilment leave insufficient surplus. The offer includes the goods/services, payment arrangement and agreement terms, not just persuasive wording (O:29–43). The author foregrounds clients and cash, then argues for market selection and differentiated value. **Project analysis:** This is evidence for offer/economics reasoning, not proof that all business constraints originate in the offer.

### core concepts

**Source:** Market pain, purchasing power, addressability and growth; premium pricing; the “Value Equation”; mapping obstacles into solutions; delivery alternatives and “Trim & Stack”; scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and naming (O:49–61, 67–93, 105–125, 137–197). The equation links a desired outcome and perceived likelihood to delay and effort. **Project analysis:** Preserve these as distinct considerations; the equation is not a calibrated quantity or an overall business score.

### workflow implications

**Source:** Select a customer/market, understand the desired outcome and obstacles, generate delivery possibilities, remove poor-value components, package the offer and test its presentation (O:105–125, 187–197). **Project analysis:** A reusable workflow needs customer evidence, delivery capacity, costs, proof and terms before recommending a stronger promise. Record commercial substance separately from its name or creative presentation.

### decision heuristics

**Source:** Commit to a niche long enough to learn; improve value rather than merely discount; start with substantial service then streamline; use real capacity limits; change creative/copy/naming before replacing a working commercial system when presentation is the problem (O:57–61, 114–117, 149–150, 194–195). The premium-price advocacy is strong, not merely neutral option comparison. **Project analysis:** Keep the conditional reasoning, not universal revenue thresholds, guaranteed success after a number of attempts or an obligation to charge more.

### metrics

**Source:** Response, booked/shown appointments, closing rate, price, upfront cash, advertising return, gross profit and lifetime gross profit (O:40–47). O explicitly distinguishes its gross-profit-based LTV from revenue-based definitions. **Project analysis:** Add clearly labelled checks for contribution after acquisition, refunds, cash timing and capacity; those checks are not all defined in the source illustration. O:47 contains an arithmetic inconsistency documented separately. The Value Equation is a qualitative model, not a numerical acceptance test.

### assumptions

**Source:** The method assumes a market with unmet needs and sufficient buying power; O:52 describes a “normal” growing market. Outcome credibility, buyer participation and a deliverable mechanism matter. **Project analysis:** Premium willingness to pay, reduced comparison and improved customer commitment are hypotheses in each context. The supplied anecdotes and illustrative figures do not establish transportable effect sizes.

### examples as abstract patterns only

**Abstracted source patterns:** A service moves from an easily compared retainer to a differentiated arrangement with explicit obligations; an extensive manual service reveals which components can be delivered more efficiently; a presentation refresh preserves an already effective offer (O:43–48, 114–125, 194–195). No original business story, sales script or numerical case is republished as a reusable example. These patterns are not newly executed customer tests.

### failure modes

**Source:** The wrong market can defeat an attractive offer; repeated niche changes discard learning; high-cost fulfilment makes refunds expensive; guarantees do not rescue poor delivery (O:49–61, 172, 183–184). **Project analysis:** Additional risks include unsupported outcome promises, inflated bonus anchors, apparent scarcity that is only a renamed promotion, excessive scope and mistaking cash collected for profit. Higher conversion does not remove these risks.

### areas of overlap

O's offer and risk-reversal reasoning overlaps P's value/pricing/sales models; its obstacle-solving offers extend into L's lead magnets and M's offer sequences. Its local presentation-change ladder complements P's intervention caution and R's distinction between tuning and changing a fundamental hypothesis. These are conceptual overlaps, not independent demonstrations of commercial effectiveness (O:105–125, 170–184, 194–195; P:145–168, 425–427; R:142–145).

### areas of tension

Premium-first positioning can conflict with learning through lower-priced early offers in M. Extensive offer construction can overrun R's minimum learning requirement. Niche commitment can conflict with evidence for a customer pivot. O's “grow or die” framing differs from P's sufficiency and maintenance. Claims of becoming incomparable do not erase P's next-best alternative (O:39–43, 57–76; M:176–178; P:152, 196–199, 451; R:67–69, 142–145).

### claims requiring broader validation

Validate causal effects of premium price on adherence, specificity on willingness to pay, guarantee types on contribution and refunds, bonus framing and scarcity on suitable purchases, and context limits of niche persistence. Do not import the book's illustrative multipliers, market-growth assertions or repeated-success rhetoric as defaults. O:4 itself labels numbers illustrative; O:47 requires a separate arithmetic correction rather than a favourable interpretation.

## 3. L: Leads extraction

### business problem addressed

**Source:** An existing offer lacks a sufficient, reliable supply of interested prospects. L explicitly builds on having an offer and distinguishes generating interest from completing a sale (L:8–11, 31–43, 64). **Project analysis:** Diagnose whether reach is actually binding; more prospect activity cannot by itself repair poor fit, delivery, retention or cash.

### core concepts

**Source:** Contactable leads versus engaged leads; a useful, narrow lead magnet; warm/cold and one-to-one/one-to-many acquisition; content that attracts and rewards attention; paid acquisition economics; “More Better New”; customer referrals, employees, agencies, affiliates and partners; effort commitments (L:41–78, 117–138, 224–248, 266–350, 365–372). **Project analysis:** Preserve engagement, qualification and customer value as separate states; do not mistake a classification of communication methods for an exhaustive business ontology.

### workflow implications

**Source:** Try the core offer directly, add a related lead magnet where useful, select acquisition work, observe results, improve a constraint, and expand or delegate proven work (L:49–72, 237–248, 296–320). **Project analysis:** Each channel hypothesis needs an intended customer, permission/authority check, downstream sales path, effort/cash budget and returned evidence. Sending messages, buying ads and mutating CRM state remain external execution.

### decision heuristics

**Source:** Give useful value before asking, favour clarity, use genuine scarcity, test presentations, do sufficient volume, improve before adding channels and use trained others for leverage (L:64–70, 129–138, 237–248, 296–320). “Rule of 100” and “Open To Goal” are explicit effort prescriptions; the latter can mean working until a daily outcome is reached (L:368–372). **Project analysis:** Adapt effort to an authorised, safe budget and a decision-relevant test. Do not adopt unlimited work, repetitive contact or spend as unconditional obligations.

### metrics

**Source:** Contacts, replies, engagement, appointments, show-up, sales, acquisition cost, lifetime gross profit, time and employee/agency cost; source discussion includes downstream conversion and retention (L:182–184, 224–232, 266–273, 303–306). The 3:1 lifetime-gross-profit/acquisition-cost ratio is expressly contextual rather than a law (L:225). **Project analysis:** Define cohort, attribution, costs and time window. L:182's multiplication does not support its stated lead yield.

### assumptions

**Source:** A relevant offer, a reachable audience, a way to deliver initial value and capability to handle responses are necessary. **Project analysis:** Interest in free material is not equivalent to budget, authority, intent or permission for unlimited follow-up. Channel capacity, labour, saturation, sales performance and downstream quality need evidence. The introductory claim that advertising can compensate for a bad product must be read alongside the book's refund and delivery problems (L:9, 15–16, 266–273).

### examples as abstract patterns only

**Abstracted source patterns:** Compare a direct commercial offer with a useful diagnostic followed by an appropriate paid service; isolate one acquisition change while keeping the rest stable; train an internal operator while maintaining the existing external route until performance is established; connect a partner's genuine customer need to a complementary offer (L:49–72, 243–244, 310–320, 328–350). These are source-derived patterns, not performed campaigns or copied promotional scripts.

### failure modes

**Source:** Unwanted magnets, insufficient useful testing, weak fulfilment that generates refunds, undiagnosed sales problems and ineffective delegation (L:44–72, 224–232, 266–273, 296–320). **Project analysis:** Cheap but unsuitable leads, uncounted founder labour, selection-biased polls, aggressive follow-up, incentive abuse and capacity overload can make apparent acquisition success commercially harmful. A calendar-based test cadence is not statistical adequacy.

### areas of overlap

Lead magnets use O's value construction and M's entry-offer logic. L's lifetime gross profit and paid growth fit R's acquisition loop, while P adds explicit qualification and all-business context. Referrals depend on delivered value, not just rewards. Testing one change and preserving productive work overlap R's measurement discipline and P's intervention caution (L:49–72, 224–248, 266–282; P:121, 211–213, 425–427; R:169–178).

### areas of tension

Volume rhetoric competes with qualification, sales/fulfilment capacity and R's retention diagnosis. Engagement polls may identify attractive wording without validating paid demand. Fast repeated outreach can conflict with permission and customer trust. Outcome-until-achieved effort can conflict with P's performance-load limits and a bounded experiment. Multiple channel expansion is not automatically better than a well-understood growth mechanism (L:64–70, 164–186, 247–248, 369–370; P:130–131, 317–318; R:171–178).

### claims requiring broader validation

Investigate typical channel response ranges, causal lead-magnet effects, referral incrementality, the transferability of activity quotas and financial ratios, attribution under multiple channels, fatigue/saturation and effects of automation on fit and permission. Book-reported portfolio results and anecdotes are not independent benchmarks. Claims concerning legal outreach rules require fresh specialist research, not source-age substitution.

## 4. M: Money Models extraction

### business problem addressed

**Source:** Customer acquisition consumes cash before enough customer receipts or profit arrive. A “Money Model” is a deliberate sequence of offers, not a complete account of everything a business does (M:22–29). The author prefers recovery within thirty days and reinvestment into further customers. **Project analysis:** Separate this financing preference from universal viability or a guarantee of unlimited growth.

### core concepts

**Source:** Attraction, upsell, downsell and continuity offers; conditional rebates; giveaways; entry/premium alternatives; quantity/free framing; pay-now/pay-later choices; complementary, menu, anchor and rollover offers; payment-plan and feature downsells; continuity bonuses, discounts and waived fees (M:26–70, 80–167). **Project analysis:** Extract customer need, timing, consideration, scope, payment and obligation changes rather than reproducing a catalogue of branded sales plays as skills.

### workflow implications

**Source:** Establish one reliable entry offer, then add further offers incrementally; make each layer financially and operationally reliable before expanding the sequence (M:174–178). **Project analysis:** For each transition capture eligibility, genuine incremental value, optional versus essential components, explicit customer acceptance, cost, cash arrival, future fulfilment, refund/credit exposure and retained value. A sequence is not permission to keep charging.

### decision heuristics

**Source:** Offer an appropriate next solution when needed; do not construct a finished multi-offer model at once; reduce scope or alter payment structure rather than indiscriminately discounting the same thing; use partners instead of creating every complementary business; start new offers at prices that permit feedback and raise them as they improve (M:28–29, 115–137, 174–178). The author also urges refunds, transparency and counsel. These cautions coexist with pressure-oriented sales instructions elsewhere, rather than cancelling their existence.

### metrics

**Source:** Acquisition cost, early customer cash/profit, take rates, payment collection, defaults/cancellations, refunds, continuation and lifetime value (M:23–24, 36–40, 68–70, 115–121, 146–167). **Project analysis:** Define whether a number is cash, sales, gross profit or contribution; distinguish commitments from collections and expected credits from refund liabilities. Thirty days is an author-selected horizon. M:158's three free months plus twelve paid months must not be shortened silently to a twelve-month total term.

### assumptions

**Source:** Complementary offers solve actual problems; customers may value alternative scope/timing; enough margin and operational reliability exist to fund the sequence (M:22–29, 175–176). **Project analysis:** Prepayments remain accompanied by future service obligations. A payment plan may cannibalise a full payment, default or increase servicing cost. Rebate qualification, later upsell uptake and renewal after discounts cannot be assumed. Access to interest-free credit is not established for every user or business by the book's illustration.

### examples as abstract patterns only

**Abstracted source patterns:** Sell an optional complementary service after a real need emerges; offer smaller scope to a customer for whom the premium scope is unsuitable; trade a clearly specified payment schedule against price; test a continuing service after proving the entry offer (M:80–109, 115–137, 174–178). Patterns retain the commercial change without copying the original scripts, industries, stories or offer bundles.

### failure modes

**Source:** A complete sequence introduced at once can overwhelm the business; bad products cannot be saved by hard selling; payment plans affect collection; unsuitable continuity creates dissatisfied customers (M:28, 115–121, 158, 166–167, 175–178). **Project analysis:** Hidden charges, misleading free/discount anchors, credit presented as cash repayment, forced upgrade choices, obstructed cancellation and optimistic refund assumptions can violate project requirements. M:36 and M:40 give different refund figures without enough comparable definitions to reconcile them numerically.

### areas of overlap

M extends O's offer/payment/guarantee work and L's entry-to-core path. P supplies whole-business cash and cost context; R supplies tests of ongoing value and appropriate growth mechanisms. The final construction chapter explicitly supports incremental learning and operational reliability, so the source cannot fairly be reduced to “maximise revenue immediately” (M:174–178; O:170–184; L:49–72; P:211–221; R:169–178).

### areas of tension

Fast cash and stronger commitments may undermine customer value, refunds, retention and future delivery. Low initial pricing differs from O's premium advocacy. Some tactics defer fees or detail, narrow choices or reward promotional acts, despite M's general transparency and voluntary-purchase principles. Recurring payment sustained by the cost of leaving is not the same evidence as voluntary repeat value. Full conflict dispositions are recorded in the reconciliation log (M:55–56, 91, 127–131, 164–167, 176–178).

### claims requiring broader validation

Validate incremental contribution from offer sequences, payback periods by model, rebate and credit behaviour, payment-plan defaults/cannibalisation, retention after discounts and waived fees, prize/price framing and long-run trust. The thirty-day credit argument, numerical cancellation thresholds and exceptional earnings are not validated universal rules. M:4–6 explicitly warns about atypical and hypothetical results. Current legality is left to qualified review.

## 5. P: Personal MBA extraction

### business problem addressed

**Source:** Build a working understanding of businesses through interrelated mental models rather than isolated tactics or credential dependence. Value creation, marketing, sales, value delivery and finance connect to people and systems (P:21–30, 60–62). **Project analysis:** Extract the relevant business reasoning; the author's business-school polemic and personal career prescriptions are not core responsibilities of this repository.

### core concepts

**Source:** Market demand, alternative forms of value, trade-offs and critical assumptions; qualification, permission and trust; several pricing methods and negotiation; value streams, throughput and quality; profit, sufficiency, acquisition allowance and cash cycles; performance load and incentives; constraints, measurement, intervention bias and resilience (P:67–108, 121–168, 171–224, 317–318, 366–367, 385–451). **Project analysis:** Use these as questions and lenses, not a universal ontology of all 271 models.

### workflow implications

**Source:** Decompose a business, identify critical assumptions, seek realistic feedback, use small offers/prototypes, observe results and improve the relevant system (P:94–108, 385–427). **Project analysis:** Connect customer, sales, delivery, cash and human constraints before accepting a local improvement. Route accounting treatment, financial advice and legal questions to their Stage 1 owners rather than implementing a general business administration suite.

### decision heuristics

**Source:** Understand probable purchasers and reject poor-fit buyers; compare alternatives; make tests honest about products still in development; preserve slack; improve constraints; avoid unnecessary intervention; define what is sufficient for the owners (P:104–108, 121, 152, 196–199, 317–318, 385–386, 425–427). P also proposes a summed market score and numerical cut-offs (P:67–69). **Project analysis:** Retain dimensional questions, not an unevidenced one-number pass/fail rule or an invented owner objective.

### metrics

**Source:** Profit/margin, revenue, financial statements, breakeven, allowable acquisition cost, lifetime value, cash timing, throughput and a small set of contextual performance indicators (P:180–181, 196–224, 407–415). P:211–213 illustrates lifetime receipts then subtracts delivery, overhead and desired profit to derive acquisition allowance. **Project analysis:** This differs from O/L's gross-profit LTV. Define the basis before comparing ratios; do not deduct delivery twice. Financial statements remain externally owned records.

### assumptions

**Source:** Models simplify; useful questions depend on context; feedback should come from plausible customers rather than only friendly supporters; a business must provide enough surplus to continue (P:28–30, 60–62, 94–108, 196–199, 413–415). **Project analysis:** Informal scores, psychology explanations and author examples are not independently calibrated tools. Statistical concepts in accessible prose do not replace a defensible sampling and inference method.

### examples as abstract patterns only

**Abstracted source patterns:** A transparent pre-order tests purchase willingness before full build; a constrained service turns away an unsuitable customer; a receivable-rich operation still lacks cash at payment dates; a local intervention is avoided because its side effects exceed its benefit (P:104–108, 121, 220–221, 425–427). These are analytical patterns, not reproduced stories or evidence that the repository has run those tests.

### failure modes

**Source:** Wrong-market effort, untested assumptions, poor qualification, unmet expectations, cash gaps, overloaded people, incentive distortion, misleading measures and intervention for its own sake (P:67–108, 121, 171–181, 220–224, 317–318, 366–367, 407–427). **Project analysis:** The breadth invites checklist theatre or a model for every situation. A score can conceal fatal dimensions; a sampling caveat cannot repair biased evidence merely by declaring a larger error margin.

### areas of overlap

P connects O's differentiated value, L's qualification and M's commercial arrangements to delivery and finance. Prototypes, critical assumptions and feedback overlap R's learning discipline; constraints and intervention caution support preservation and repair. P's mental-model form supplies vocabulary, not a replacement for the specialised workflows (P:94–108, 145–168, 211–221, 385–427; R:67–69, 188–191).

### areas of tension

Owner-defined sufficiency and maintenance conflict with universal growth imperatives. A “Minimum Viable Offer” that secures a transaction is not identical to every R learning experiment. The market score conflicts with keeping quality dimensions separate. Several pricing/negotiation lenses are broader than an always-premium or never-negotiate rule. Performance load qualifies L's open-ended effort prescription (P:67–69, 107, 145–156, 196–199, 317–318, 451; L:369–370; R:81–84).

### claims requiring broader validation

Review the market-score cut-offs, pricing and guarantee generalisations, psychological/incentive mechanisms, sampling/error explanations and claims concerning optimal intervention or growth cycles. Validate specific models against their original research rather than counting this synthesis as independent confirmation. Exclude career/education advocacy from the capability core. Preserve accounting and regulated-finance boundaries even where the book discusses those topics educationally.

## 6. R: Lean Startup extraction

### business problem addressed

**Source:** Under extreme uncertainty, executing a detailed plan efficiently can still build the wrong business; claimed learning can disguise failure. Startups need validated learning and an appropriate management discipline (R:30–50). The definition is not a synonym for every small or newly incorporated business. **Project analysis:** Distinguish uncertainty-reducing discovery from repeatable execution and from an owner's choice to maintain a sufficient business.

### core concepts

**Source:** Value and growth hypotheses; Build-Measure-Learn; planning backwards from the learning need; minimum viable products; innovation accounting; cohort and split-test evidence; actionable, accessible and auditable metrics; pivot or persevere; small batches; sticky, viral and paid growth engines; Five Whys and an innovation sandbox (R:53–69, 81–123, 134–145, 151–153, 168–178, 188–191, 211–213).

### workflow implications

**Source:** Identify assumptions, decide what must be learned and measured, build the least effort needed for that test, establish a baseline, tune, and review whether the underlying hypothesis should change (R:67–69, 99–101, 135–145). **Project analysis:** Preserve the observation, interpretation and decision separately. An interest test cannot alone establish delivery or retention. The eventual experiment contract must support an inconclusive result, not manufacture a binary success verdict from noisy data.

### decision heuristics

**Source:** Test risky assumptions early; measure the whole learning loop; favour small batches; do not confuse activity or aggregate growth with learning; use a structured pivot review; make proportional investments in prevention; bound experiments and monitor harm (R:99–123, 135–145, 151–153, 188–191, 211–213). **Project analysis:** Preserve validated layers unless implicated, but do not forbid a larger change supported by evidence. R's own redesign account shows that endless small optimisation can miss a needed strategic correction (R:141–142).

### metrics

**Source:** Cohort conversion, activation, retention/churn, viral coefficient, acquisition cost and variable-cost-adjusted lifetime value; learning milestones compare baselines and subsequent results (R:99–123, 169–178). **Project analysis:** A cohort is not automatically a randomised test. Define exposure, denominator, observation period, costs and uncertainty. Engine formulas omit important cash/capacity conditions if copied as sufficient scaling gates. The R:103 chart supports the contrast with aggregate growth, not exact digit-level estimates read from a drawing.

### assumptions

**Source:** Relevant customers can encounter a test, reactions can be measured, and learning can guide decisions; early adopters may tolerate a different product from mainstream customers. R acknowledges quality, intellectual-property and reputation concerns (R:81–95). **Project analysis:** The test must preserve enough fidelity to answer its question. Sparse samples, network effects, long buying cycles, safety obligations or poor instrumentation can prevent cheap/fast evidence from being adequate. These are validation conditions, not excuses to skip research.

### examples as abstract patterns only

**Abstracted source patterns:** Manual delivery probes whether customers value a service before automation; a cohort comparison reveals that acquisition growth has hidden flat per-customer performance; a removed onboarding step is tested against the existing path; bounded exposure permits a strategic redesign while preserving a comparison (R:53–57, 88–91, 103–121, 141–142, 211–213). The book includes concealed human-assisted delivery; that is recorded as a source tactic, not silently rewritten as transparent practice.

### failure modes

**Source:** Success theatre, vanity metrics, premature infrastructure, confirmation bias, slow learning batches, inability to pivot, loss of focus on a clear hypothesis, blame-oriented root-cause analysis and politically contested data (R:37–50, 103–123, 134–145, 188–191, 208–209). **Project analysis:** Too-weak tests can produce false rejection; enthusiastic early users need not represent later buyers; repeated unplanned tests can overstate evidence. A compelling learning story is not independent causal proof.

### areas of overlap

R's manual tests and evolving delivery overlap O's service learning and P's prototypes. Its paid engine connects to L/M's acquisition and customer economics. Retention diagnosis, evidence-led iteration and proportional repair strengthen whole-business evaluation. R:226–229 explicitly calls for further methodological research and warns against doctrine, supporting rather than replacing the bootstrap's independent-validation requirement.

### areas of tension

Rapid persuasive optimisation may not test the actual value hypothesis. Niche persistence can require reversal when customer evidence changes. Revenue or growth is insufficient proof of learning. A successful modest business may fail a venture-growth objective without being an intrinsically bad business (R:88, 125–127). Concealed human delivery conflicts with material-truthfulness requirements; sandbox team autonomy does not grant this agent authority to spend, publish or access customers. Very short tests do not necessarily provide reliable inference (R:88–91, 211–213).

### claims requiring broader validation

Research effects and boundary conditions of hypothesis-driven entrepreneurship, early prototypes, cohorts and controlled tests, small-batch work, growth-engine diagnosis, pivot timing and Five Whys. The source provides cases and theory, not a universal causal proof or success guarantee. Its invitation to compare alternative methods (R:226–229) belongs in Stage 3's research agenda. Do not turn historical company outcomes or the proposed stock-exchange discussion into current facts.

## 7. Outputs and handoff

The [source-to-capability matrix](2026-09-09-stage-02-source-to-capability-matrix.md) converts the extracted ideas into individually traceable candidate responsibilities. The [reconciliation and taxonomy](2026-09-09-stage-02-reconciliation-and-taxonomy.md) contains overlaps, conflicts, dispositions, metric corrections and the broader-validation queue. [Conformance](2026-09-09-stage-02-conformance.md) checks all five sources and all eleven fields against original §9.

No book-derived rule is promoted to empirical truth by this stage. Stage 3 receives source-backed hypotheses, explicit tensions and research questions. It must research professional and empirical practice independently, including criticisms and business-model limits; it must not simply count repeated recommendations across these books.
